Havanese Potty Tray to Outdoor: How to Teach Both (and Fix a Havanese That Won't Pee Outside)

A Havanese potty tray can be a lifesaver in a flat, in a snowy winter, or on a long workday. Then comes the hard part. You want your dog to go outside too, and your little Havanese walks around the block, comes home, and pees on the tray. Or on the rug. This guide compares six indoor setups by one question that most product lists skip: how easily does the habit move outdoors? Then it shows how to keep both spots working, and how to reset a dog that just won’t pee outside.

Quick answer: Pick an indoor surface that feels like the outdoor one (grass tray or sod box if your dog will mostly go on grass). Give each spot its own cue word. Move the tray toward the door in small steps, then put it outside, then shrink it. Make outdoor trips short, boring “potty only” trips, and reward the second your dog finishes. Do not just take the tray away overnight.

Why does the surface matter so much? Dogs build a habit around what they feel under their paws and what they smell. A puppy who learned on a soft, flat pad may think “soft flat thing” is the toilet. That is why so many owners see their dog pick the bath mat once the pad is gone. The fix is not more walks. It is a bridge from one surface to the next.

Mini-reviews: six indoor setups and how they hand off to outdoors

What should you expect from each setup in real life? Below, each one gets the same four checks: who it suits, how it feels to the dog, how hard it is to move outside, and your next step.

1. Flat disposable pad in a holder

This is the classic puppy pad, clipped into a plastic frame so it does not slide. It is cheap and easy. The catch is that it feels like a rug. Many Havanese owners who report rug accidents started here.

Specs and feel

Soft, flat, absorbent top. Some pads carry a scent that draws puppies in. Holder keeps edges flat and stops chewing.

Pros: fast cleanup, no smell build-up if changed often, easy to move a little each day. Cons: lowest likeness to grass, can teach “soft floor = toilet.” Next step: use it for the first weeks, then switch to a grass top or start moving it toward the door.

2. Grid tray with a pad underneath

A plastic grate sits over a pad, so paws stay dry and the pad can’t be shredded. Some dogs love it. Some hate it. In an r/Havanese thread about teaching the potty bell and the indoor tray together, one owner said their Havi refused the grid tray and would only use a large flat pad on the floor, while another described a friend’s bell-trained Havanese who switched happily to an artificial-grass box beside the deck.

Specs and feel

Hard plastic grid, often with a raised edge or post for males. Pad sits below the grid. Grid needs rinsing.

Pros: dry paws, no shredding, clean look. Cons: the grid feel is unlike anything outside; some dogs avoid it. Next step: if your dog stalls on the grid, lay a pad on top for a week, then go back to the grid or move to a grass top.

3. Artificial grass tray

A frame with a removable turf mat over a drip tray. For most Havanese homes that want to keep a tray for good, this is the best middle ground. The blades feel close to a lawn, so the habit carries over.

Specs and feel

Turf mat on a grate, tray below. Mat must be rinsed and dried often, or it starts to smell.

Pros: good carry-over to grass, lasts years, works in a sunroom or balcony. Cons: more cleaning than a pad; long coat can pick up wet turf. Next step: this is the tray to buy if you want to teach both spots.

4. Real grass or sod box

A box with live or cut grass that you replace when it wears out. Humane World for Animals lists a sod box next to pee pads and newspaper as indoor options for times your puppy is alone. It is the closest thing to the real lawn.

Specs and feel

Real grass on a liner. Needs regular replacing. Best in a bright spot such as a balcony.

Pros: highest carry-over, easy to place by the door and then outside. Cons: ongoing cost and mess; not great in a dark flat. Next step: use it as the “bridge” for the last weeks before outdoor-only.

5. Litter box with paper pellets

Some puppies come home already used to a box, so ask your breeder what surface the litter used. It suits small dogs in high-rise flats where outdoor trips take time.

Specs and feel

Low-entry box, paper or wood pellets made for dogs. Scoop daily.

Pros: contains mess, long-term indoor option. Cons: pellets are nothing like grass, so the move outside is the hardest from here. Next step: if outdoor potty is the goal, sprinkle a few pellets on the outdoor spot at first so it smells familiar.

6. Washable pad on the floor

A fabric pad you throw in the washer. Less waste than disposables, and some dogs that refuse grids accept it.

Specs and feel

Quilted, absorbent fabric with a waterproof back. Needs two or three in rotation.

Pros: low waste, soft, dogs like it. Cons: looks and feels like a bath mat, which is exactly the confusion you want to avoid. Next step: keep it as a weather-day backup only, and pick up loose rugs while you train.

How to teach both a potty tray and outdoor potty

Can one small dog really use two toilets? Yes, if each spot has clear rules. The owner in that Midwest thread had a 5-month-old who rang the door bells reliably and used the tray only when nobody heard the bell. That is a good start. The goal is to make the tray a choice you offer, not a spot the dog guesses at.

Give each spot its own cue

Say a short phrase while your dog goes. Humane World for Animals suggests a phrase like “go potty” so the dog links the words with the action. For two spots, use two phrases: “go potty” outside and “tray” at the tray. Walk your dog to the tray on a weather day and use only the tray word.

Put the tray near the outdoor door

Keep the tray in the same room as the door you use for outdoor trips. That way the bell, the door and the tray are part of one routine. A tray in a far bedroom becomes a separate habit.

Reward both, on a schedule

Reward the second your dog finishes, every time, at either spot. Feed at the same times each day. Humane World notes that a steady feeding schedule makes house training much easier. Keep a simple note of when your dog goes, and you will see a pattern within days.

Add the bell after the outdoor spot works

Certified trainer Cathy Madson at Preventive Vet suggests adding a potty bell only once the outdoor spot is learned, and opening the door right away when the dog rings it. Ring-and-go is the reward. If you also want the tray, the bell means “outside”; the tray is always open, no bell needed.

Tray-to-outdoor transition, step by step

Ready to retire the tray, or at least use it less? This order is the core of most trainer advice, and it matches what Preventive Vet describes.

  1. Do not just take the tray away. Preventive Vet warns that removing the pad or grass patch all at once can send your dog to a bath mat or hallway rug instead.
  2. Move it toward the door. Each day, move it about a foot or two closer to the outdoor door, until it sits right by the door.
  3. Interrupt kindly. When your dog walks toward the tray, open the door and go out together instead.
  4. Put the tray outside. Set it just outside the door. Your dog now goes outdoors, on a familiar surface.
  5. Shrink it. Cut the pad smaller or roll back the turf a little at a time, until your dog is on the ground.
  6. Cut extra trays. If you have more than one, bring them toward the main one and then remove them. Gate off other floors while you train.

How often should a puppy go out during all this? Humane World for Animals gives a rule of thumb: a puppy may hold their bladder for about one hour per month of age, and should go out after waking, after play, and after eating or drinking. The same page warns that indoor potty training can make outdoor house training take longer, which is why the bridge steps matter.

Small size matters here. A Havanese bladder is small, and adults vary a lot in size. If you are curious where your adult dog sits, see our real owner weights compared with the breed standard.

How to fix a Havanese that won’t pee outside

Why would a dog walk for half an hour and then pee the minute you get home? Because to that dog, outside means “walk” and home means “toilet.” It is one of the most common posts in Havanese owner groups: many trips out, almost no outdoor pees, then an accident at home. The replies tend to repeat the same few fixes. Here they are as a plan.

Split the potty trip from the walk

Go to one spot. Stand still. Let your dog sniff on a short leash. No play, no walk, no new smells. Say the cue. If your dog goes, reward and then start the walk. The walk becomes the prize for peeing, not the thing that ends after it.

If nothing happens, try again soon

After five minutes with no result, go back in and keep your dog close: on a leash attached to you, in a crate, or in a small pen. Try again in 10 to 15 minutes. This removes the chance to sneak off to the rug.

Bring a familiar smell outside

Place a used pad or a piece of the turf mat on the outdoor spot. The smell says “this is the place.” Preventive Vet also mentions attractant sprays for the outdoor area if the indoor pad had one built in.

Clean and clear the indoor spots

Clean accidents with an enzyme cleaner so no smell is left. Pick up loose rugs and bath mats for a few weeks. If your dog prefers rugs, every rug is a pad in your dog’s eyes.

Watch the signs

Sniffing the floor, circling, pacing, going to the door or whining mean “now.” Take your dog straight to the right spot. When you can’t watch, use a crate or pen. A dog that is calm alone in a pen is easier to train; our guide on when Havanese puppy clinginess eases covers building that alone time.

Weather plays a part too. In the tray thread above, one owner says their Havi uses the floor pad on long days or in bad weather because he hates rain on his fur. A wet coat that is not dried well tangles fast, so towel your dog after rainy potty trips. Our guide to preventing mats during coat change explains why damp fur mats so quickly. And if a dog who was fully trained starts having many accidents out of nowhere, talk to your vet.

Alternatives worth a look and buying tips

What if none of the six setups fits? A few owners build a simple outdoor box with artificial grass next to a deck, so the dog has one fixed outdoor spot and the lawn stays clean. Others use a puppy pen around the tray for the first months. When you buy, check three things: a low entry edge your puppy can step over, a turf or grid you can rinse in your sink or shower, and a size big enough for your dog to turn around. Buy two turf mats so one can dry while the other is in use.

Keep management simple for the rest of the house, too. If your dog barks at the door every time someone passes on the way to the tray, our calm plan for window and doorbell barking can make that corner quieter.

FAQ

Can a Havanese use a potty tray and go outside?

Yes. Keep the tray near the outdoor door, use a different cue word for each spot, and reward both. Many owners keep a tray for bad weather and long days while the dog goes outside the rest of the time.

Why does my Havanese pee inside right after a walk?

Your dog may see walks as walks, not potty time. Make short, boring potty trips to one spot first, reward the moment your dog goes, and start the fun walk only after that.

Should I just take the pads away?

No. Removing the pad or grass patch all at once can push a dog to rugs and bath mats. Move the tray toward the door, then outside, then shrink it.

Which tray is best for moving to outdoor potty?

A grass surface. Real grass or a sod box feels closest to a lawn, and an artificial grass tray is a good long-term middle ground.

How often should a Havanese puppy go out?

Humane World for Animals gives a rule of thumb of about one hour of holding time per month of age, and suggests trips after waking, play, eating and drinking.

When should I add potty bells?

After your dog already goes outside reliably. Open the door right away when the bell rings so the bell clearly means “outside.”

My Havanese hates the grid tray. What now?

Lay a pad or turf on top of the grid for a week, or switch to a flat pad or grass tray. Some Havanese owners report their dog simply refuses the grid feel.

Final verdict: your perfect potty plan

So what is the best Havanese potty tray plan? If you want both spots, buy an artificial grass tray, put it by the outdoor door, and give it its own cue. If you want outdoor-only, use a sod box as the last bridge and move it outside step by step. If your dog won’t pee outside, split the potty trip from the walk and keep the indoor options small and clean. Use the planner at the top of this page to pick your first step today.
