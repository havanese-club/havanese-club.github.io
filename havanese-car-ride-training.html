Havanese Whining and Lap-Jumping in the Car: A Step-by-Step Car Ride Training Plan

Published 2026-11-01

Havanese whining and lap-jumping in the car are two of the most common complaints from owners of this little companion breed, and both are learnable skills to fix, not fixed parts of your dog’s personality. A dog bred to stay close to people does not see why the driver’s lap should be off limits. The good news: with short, planned sessions you can teach a calm “back seat” habit one small rung at a time.

Quick answer: Stop the lap-jumping first by giving your Havanese one fixed spot, such as a crate or a car harness clipped to a seat-belt tether, practiced in a parked car. Then build calm in small steps: near the car, inside with the door open, door closed, engine on, end of the driveway, around the block, a short trip to somewhere fun. Move up only when your dog stays loose and keeps taking treats. If the whining gets louder, go back one step. Never reward whining with attention, but do reward the first quiet moment.

Where is your Havanese on the car ride ladder? Session tracker

Pick the rung your dog can already do calmly, then log each short session. The tracker tells you when to move up, stay or step back. It is a training notebook, not a behavior diagnosis.

How did today’s session go?

    Why a ladder? Shelter trainers use the same idea. The Toronto Humane Society car travel guide says that if a dog won’t walk within 4 feet of the car, you start farther away, maybe at 10 feet, and close the gap over later sessions. The same sheet gives the sign that you moved too fast: your dog stops taking treats or playing and gets a tense body or tucked tail. Our “three calm sessions in a row” rule to move up is an editorial rule of thumb, not a number from that guide. It just keeps you from rushing.

    The video below is from professional trainer Terrie Hayward. She walks through a five-part plan using desensitization and counter-conditioning: building a happy place in the car, a “car good stuff” game, a car step game, getting used to sounds and small movements, and then the smallest rides. Watch it before your first session so you can see what “small steps” look like.

    Why does my Havanese whine in the car?

    Most car whining comes from one of four things: excitement about where you are going, worry about the car itself, a learned habit that once got attention, or a real need such as a potty break. The fix depends on which one you are dealing with, so watch when the whining starts.

    The Havanese is a small companion dog. Wikipedia’s Havanese entry lists a weight of about 7 to 14 lb and describes the breed’s “friendly and readily trained nature,” noting that these dogs became “devoted companions” to Spanish colonists in Cuba. That history explains a lot. A dog bred to sit with people wants to be where you are, and in a car that means your lap. If you are curious how your own dog compares with the breed range, see our page on real Havanese owner weights vs the standard.

    Excited whining

    Starts when the car turns onto a familiar road, or right before you arrive at the park. The dog is bright-eyed, bouncy and looks out of the window. This is anticipation. It often grows if every ride ends somewhere thrilling.

    Worried whining

    Starts before the car moves, or the moment the engine starts. Look for a low tail, ears back, yawning, lip licking, trembling or a dog that refuses treats. This is fear. The Toronto Humane Society says fear is usually at the root of a dog’s reluctance about the car, from a past bad experience or just the size, sounds and movement of the vehicle.

    Attention whining

    Starts when you stop talking to your dog, and stops when you look back or say “shh, it’s okay.” Each time the noise gets you to turn around, the dog learns that whining works. Small, vocal dogs pick this up fast. If your Havanese also barks at the door at home, our calm plan for window and doorbell barking uses the same idea: reward the quiet moment, not the noise.

    Need-based whining

    Starts later in a longer ride and comes with restless circling or sniffing the seat. It may simply mean “I need to go.” Give a potty stop before every training ride so you can tell real needs apart from habit. If your dog is still learning where to go, our potty tray to outdoor guide helps you build a reliable pre-ride potty routine.

    If your dog seems queasy rather than excited or worried, that is a question for your vet, not for a training plan. This article only covers the behavior side.

    How do I stop my Havanese from jumping into my lap while driving?

    Give your dog one fixed riding spot and teach it in a parked car before you ever drive. A crate, or a car harness clipped to a seat-belt tether, takes lap-jumping off the menu so you only have to train calm, not chase a moving target.

    In a Dog Trick Academy forum thread about a dog that cried and climbed onto laps in the car, one member advised never letting the dog sit on a lap, especially in the front, and said that harnessing and buckling the dog in reduced both the whining and the moving around. Another member suggested clicking and treating calm behavior in a parked car first, then in a moving car. That is the order this plan follows.

    Shelter trainers agree on the gear. The Toronto Humane Society guide says car travel training should include getting your dog used to a car harness or, even better, a crate, because it is not safe to drive with an unsecured dog if you have to brake hard. Writing in Whole Dog Journal, trainer Mardi Richmond puts it simply: a crate or safety restraint is “both more safe and more sane.”

Whatever you pick, practice the harness or crate at home first. Whole Dog Journal notes that a dog new to a harness needs practice away from the car before you buckle up.

    Set the rule on day one

    From the first parked session, your dog rides in that spot every time. No “just this once” on the lap. A single lap ride at a red light teaches your Havanese that climbing can still pay off, and you are back to square one.

    The step-by-step car ride training plan

    This plan combines the Toronto Humane Society sequence and the five steps in Whole Dog Journal’s “Road Scholar” article. Keep sessions short, end on a good note, and do not skip ahead because one session went well.

    1. Near the car (engine off). Stand at a distance where your dog is relaxed. Feed treats or play a little game, then walk away. If your dog won’t go near the car, start farther away, as the Toronto Humane Society describes (for example 10 feet instead of 4).
    2. Door open, treats inside. Lay a trail of treats across the seat to the far side, so your dog can’t reach them all just by stretching in. A stuffed food toy on the far side keeps your dog in longer. Don’t force your dog in; keep the door open so they don’t feel trapped.
    3. In the riding spot, door closed. Clip the harness or close the crate. Sit with your dog for a few minutes with the doors closed and reward calm. Whole Dog Journal suggests sitting together briefly, getting out, and repeating several times.
    4. Engine on, car in park. Run the engine for a minute or two while you reward calm, then turn it off and let your dog out. The Toronto Humane Society suggests tossing treats into the back or letting your dog lick peanut butter from a wooden spoon while the engine runs.
    5. End of the driveway and back. The first “ride” is tiny. Whole Dog Journal says some dogs need to start with half a block, then a full block.
    6. Around the block. Give your dog a long-lasting food toy so you can keep your eyes on the road.
    7. A short trip to somewhere fun. Drive to a nearby park and finish with a walk, so the ride ends with a reward.
    8. Longer and duller trips. Add distance slowly and mix in boring errands, so the car stops meaning “big excitement.”

    How long will it take? Whole Dog Journal says some dogs progress in a few sessions, while others need a few sessions a day for several days or even weeks. The Toronto Humane Society says it depends entirely on how afraid the dog is. Use the tracker at the top of this page to see your own pattern instead of guessing.

    Rewarding quiet without rewarding whining

    Wait for a pause in the noise, even two seconds, then mark it with a calm “yes” and drop a treat in the crate or on the seat. Say nothing while your dog whines. You are teaching that quiet makes good things happen. A passenger can hand out treats while you drive; if you drive alone, a long-lasting food toy does the job.

    Keep the other rides out of the way

    Whole Dog Journal advises avoiding vet visits and trips to the kennel or groomer during this process. That is hard with a coated breed, so plan grooming around your training weeks. Our at-home Havanese grooming schedule can help you stretch the time between salon trips.

    What if my Havanese only cries once the car is moving?

    Go back to the last rung where your dog was calm, often “engine on, car in park,” and add motion in tiny pieces: roll a few feet, stop, reward, park. Movement is a new trigger, so it gets its own small steps.

    Many owners can sit happily in a parked car with their dog and still hear crying the second the wheels turn. That does not mean the earlier work failed. It means your dog learned “parked car is fine” and now needs to learn “moving car is fine.” Repeat the driveway step until your dog stays loose, and keep the first moving sessions under a minute.

    Help your dog settle on the move

    • Use a food toy your dog only gets in the car, so the car becomes the place for the best treat.
    • Do a potty break and a short sniff walk before the ride, so your dog gets in a little tired.
    • Keep your voice low and boring. Cheerful chatter can wind a Havanese up.
    • Drive gently: slow turns and soft stops are easier on a small dog learning to balance.

    Is it separation-type worry or just excitement?

    If the whining starts as soon as you pick up the keys or open the car door, and gets worse when one person leaves the car at a stop, your Havanese may be worried about being apart from you. That needs different practice than excited whining.

    A Havanese who whines when the driver gets out to pay for fuel is not upset about the car. It is upset about the empty seat. Build short “you stay, I step away” practice at home first, then in the parked car with a second person nearby. Our guide on when Havanese puppy clinginess eases covers simple alone-time steps you can carry into the car. For excited whining, the fix is the opposite: make the car boring. Take short rides that end at home, sit in the parked car with a chew, or drive to the shop and back before you go to the park.

    Common mistakes that keep the whining going

    Most stalled plans fail for the same few reasons. Check this list before you decide your dog “just whines.”

    • Turning around to soothe. “It’s okay, baby” every time your dog cries pays off the noise. Reward the quiet gap instead.
    • Allowing the lap “just at red lights.” Every lap ride keeps the climbing habit alive.
    • Moving up too fast. If your dog stops taking treats, you went too far. The Toronto Humane Society says to go back to a point where your dog feels comfortable again.
    • Only driving to big fun or big stress. If every ride ends at the dog park or the groomer, the car predicts excitement or worry. Mix in dull trips.
    • Training on a full stomach. Whole Dog Journal notes that a belly full of treats can make some dogs feel worse, so keep food small and save the long engine-running sessions for another day if your dog seems uneasy.
    • Skipping the parked-car steps. Driving off on the first day turns the whole car into the trigger. Calm in park comes first.
    • Letting your dog jump out on their own. Whole Dog Journal says dogs should be taught not to jump out of the car without your permission. Ask for a sit and use a release word before the door opens.

    Before-every-ride checklist

    Use this short routine for each training ride. It takes about five minutes and makes the session more likely to go well.

    • Potty break and a short sniff walk.
    • Harness on, or crate ready, with a familiar blanket.
    • Small treats and one long-lasting food toy within reach for you or a passenger.
    • Decide the rung: today’s step, and the step below it if things go badly.
    • Plan a fun ending: a short walk, a game, or a calm chew at home.
    • Log the result in the tracker above when you are back.

    Weather adds its own chores for a long-coated dog. If your Havanese gets wet on the way back from the park, towel and dry the coat well; our guide to preventing mats during coat change explains why damp fur tangles fast.

    FAQ

    Why does my Havanese whine in the car but not at home?

    The car brings new sounds, movement and sometimes worry or excitement. Watch when the whining starts: before the car moves usually points to worry, near favorite places to excitement, and when you stop talking to attention.

    Should I let my Havanese sit on my lap while I drive?

    No. Give your dog one fixed spot, such as a crate or a car harness on a seat-belt tether, and use it every ride. Shelter trainers say driving with an unsecured dog is not safe if you have to brake hard.

    How long does car ride training take?

    It depends on the dog. Whole Dog Journal says some dogs progress in a few sessions, while others need a few short sessions a day for several days or even weeks. Move up only when your dog is calm at the current step.

    Should I ignore the whining completely?

    Ignore the noise, but don’t ignore your dog. Reward the first short quiet moment with a calm “yes” and a treat. If your dog stops taking treats, the step is too hard, so go back one rung.

    Is a crate or a harness better for stopping lap-jumping?

    Both stop lap-jumping if used every ride. A crate suits dogs already relaxed in one. A harness with a short seat-belt tether suits dogs who settle better when they can see their people. Practice either one at home first.

    My Havanese is fine parked but cries once we drive. What now?

    Treat movement as a new step. Go back to engine-on in park, then roll a few feet, stop and reward. Keep first moving sessions under a minute and build up slowly.

    Your next step

    Havanese whining and lap-jumping in the car usually fade when you fix the spot first and then build calm one small step at a time. Pick a crate or harness, find the rung your dog can already do, and log today’s five-minute session in the tracker at the top. Each calm session is a skill your dog is learning, and the plan gets easier as you both practice.
